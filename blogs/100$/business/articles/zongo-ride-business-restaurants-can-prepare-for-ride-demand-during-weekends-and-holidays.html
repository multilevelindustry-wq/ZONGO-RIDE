A repeatable workflow makes the transport option easier to use and easier to manage. Make the customer or employee experience clear

If a customer has a mobility requirement, needs assistance with luggage or is travelling with children, encourage them to ask the driver or provider specific questions before confirming. Coordinate people, drivers and partners

Clear coordination reduces avoidable waiting and misunderstandings. Give each journey one primary contact and make sure that person knows what they are authorised to change. Plan costs and commercial value

Treat additional revenue as a hypothesis to test, not an automatic result of offering rides. Easier access may help a customer attend an appointment or visit a shop, but the business still needs to measure whether the customer came, completed a purchase and returned. Estimate the full cost of the journey before making claims about savings or business value. Include the fare, any booking or platform charges that apply, staff time spent arranging the trip, waiting time, cancellation exposure and the cost of an alternative if the ride is unavailable. Use pricing and payment rules transparently

Price clarity protects trust. Before a passenger accepts a journey, they should understand the fare or how the fare will be determined, who is paying and whether extra waiting, stops or route changes could affect the total. A business should avoid promising a fixed price unless the provider has confirmed it. Record what changes for the passenger, the coordinator, the driver and the person paying.

Track measures such as guest satisfaction, arrival conversion, repeat visits, booking completion and support contacts per journey. Separate enquiries from accepted requests and completed journeys; a large number of clicks or messages is not proof of commercial value.

Choose useful performance measures

Compare results with a baseline and consider differences in location, day, season and customer type. A pilot that runs during a quiet week may not predict a major event period. A route with frequent traffic disruption should not be compared casually with a short off-peak journey. Run a limited pilot before scaling

A pilot helps a business learn without committing to a large rollout. Choose one location, team, customer group or journey category; define the start and end dates; set a spending limit; and name the person responsible for the result. Tell participants what the pilot is testing and what it is not testing. Use feedback from the people who complete the journey to refine the process before expanding it.

At the end of the pilot, review both positive and negative evidence. Count completed journeys, late arrivals, cancellations, complaints, cost and staff time. Give the pilot a named owner, a defined audience, a review date, a spending ceiling and a stop-or-adjust rule. A small trial is not proof that the model will work in every location or season.

Illustrative example: applying the idea in an operating setting

Consider a small hotel sharing practical arrival guidance with guests. This is a hypothetical example, not a Zongo Ride customer result or a forecast. The manager records the current process: how people ask for travel information, how many enquiries become completed journeys, how much staff time is spent answering questions and which problems recur. The manager then tests a limited ride-information workflow for one journey type. Participants receive a single message with the location, meeting point, timing, fare-confirmation reminder and a contact for changes. The business does not promise that a driver will always be available; it asks the passenger to confirm the trip and keeps another option for critical appointments.

At review, the team compares actual completed journeys with the baseline. Did people understand the instructions? Were requests accepted at the required times? Did the process create more work for reception? Were costs recorded accurately? Did any passenger need a service an ordinary ride could not provide? If the evidence is positive, the team can refine the instructions and test another route. If it is weak, the team can stop or redesign the trial before making a larger commitment. This evidence-led approach is more credible than assuming that a new transport option automatically creates growth.

A practical 30-day implementation roadmap

Days 1–5: define the use case

Interview the people affected by how restaurants can prepare for ride demand during weekends and holidays. Gather examples of missed, delayed or difficult journeys; identify the locations and time windows involved; and define a successful outcome. Confirm whether an ordinary ride is appropriate or whether a specialist or contracted provider is required.

Days 6–10: validate service and responsibilities

Check local coverage and transport requirements. Verify what the marketplace supports in the target area, how driver acceptance is confirmed, how fares are communicated and what the fallback will be. Assign a process owner and document who approves, pays, communicates and handles complaints.

Days 11–20: run a limited pilot

Start with a small group and a defined budget. Provide plain-language instructions, record requests and outcomes, and collect feedback from passengers, coordinators and drivers. Do not expand during the measurement period unless a safety or operational issue requires a change.

Days 21–30: review and decide

Compare the pilot with the baseline using guest satisfaction, arrival conversion, repeat visits, booking completion and support contacts per journey. Record direct and indirect costs, unresolved issues and assumptions that proved wrong. Decide whether to stop, adjust or extend the test, and document the evidence behind that decision.

Decision checklist before committing money or making a promise

  • The business need and intended passenger group are clearly defined.
  • The route, time, fare and driver acceptance can be confirmed before the journey.
  • The business has checked local rules, insurance and contractual responsibilities.
  • Passengers know how to ask questions, change a request or report a problem.
  • Personal information is collected only when necessary and handled appropriately.
  • The budget includes staff time, waiting, cancellations and alternative transport.
  • The team has a baseline and can measure completed journeys and business outcomes.
  • No one is promising guaranteed bookings, guaranteed income or investment returns.
